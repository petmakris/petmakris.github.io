The

👋 🛒 ☕ 🐕
Four theatrical sessions of ninety minutes for Lila, seven years old, starting from no English at all. Every session ends with a scene the two of you perform — both of you playing both roles.
What is inside

Teacher sheets with the Greek meanings for you, word cards, full scripts in large type, three games per session, cut-out props — play money, a menu, an order pad, prescriptions, show tickets — and a diploma.

How to print

A4, single-sided, colour if you can, "background graphics" switched on. Print the whole pack once. Card pages and prop pages are meant to be cut along the dashed lines; keep everything in one box that only comes out at English time.

Before you startRules

Eight rules
for the grown-up

You are not a teacher and she is not a student. You are two actors with a hat. Read this page once before session one; it matters more than any of the word lists.

  1. The hat is the switch.When you put the hat on, English begins. When it comes off, English stops. A 90-minute lesson feels like homework; a hat that turns you into someone else feels like a game. Same 90 minutes.
  2. The Greek Button rescues, it does not replace.Cut out the red button on the props page. She may slap it any time and get ten seconds of Greek — then the hat is back on. Knowing the escape exists is what stops the panic that makes children refuse to speak.
  3. Never correct inside a scene.If she says "I are happy", you answer in character: "You are happy! I am happy too!" She hears the right form without being stopped. Correcting mid-scene teaches her that speaking is dangerous.
  4. Play every scene three times.Run 1 — you play both roles with a toy while she only watches and listens. Run 2 — she takes role A, reading or repeating after you. Run 3 — you swap. The third run is where the language actually sticks.
  5. Three to five words per line. Always the same words.Repetition is not boring for a beginner, it is the entire lesson. She needs to hear "How much is it?" thirty times, not thirty different questions.
  6. Silly beats perfect.A monster voice, a wrong price of one hundred francs, a customer who pays with a sock. If she laughs, she remembers. If she is bored, nothing sticks however correct it was.
  7. Stop a block early, never late.The moment her eyes drift, end that block and move to a movement game, even if five minutes are left. Ending on a high note is what makes her want the next session.
  8. Your accent does not matter.You are not the model of perfect English, you are her playmate in English. A parent playing enthusiastically in imperfect English beats a perfect recording she ignores.

Before you startRituals

The two chants
and the card games

These four things never change from session one to session four. Everything else in the pack is new each time; these are the walls she leans on.

The opening chant

Hello, hello, what's your name?
My name is ________ , what's your name?
Nice to meet you! Nice to meet you!
Let's play, let's play, let's play in English!

The closing chant

Goodbye, goodbye, see you soon!
Goodbye, goodbye, that was fun!
One more star, one more day —
Bye-bye, bye-bye, hip hooray!

Clap on every stressed syllable and march on the spot. By session three she will start the chant herself before you do — that is the moment you know the ritual has taken.

Four ways to use any set of word cards

1 · Show and say

Hold one up, say it, she repeats. All twelve, twice. Fast, no explanations, no writing.

2 · Grab it

Cards face up on the floor. You say a word, she slaps the card. Then she says a word and you slap — losing on purpose is the point.

3 · What is missing?

She shuts her eyes, you steal a card. She names the missing one in English. Start with six cards, not twelve.

4 · Hide and seek

Hide the cards round the room. Each one she finds, she shouts its name before she may keep it.

Before you startStar chart

Lila's star chart

Six stars per session, one for each block she finishes. Colour a star in front of her, out loud, and say why. Stars are given for trying, never for being correct — the day you withhold a star for a mistake is the day the pack stops working.

Three things worth knowing before you start

  • Do not teach "I am" versus "I'm". Teach only "I'm". Rules come years later.
  • The numbers chant (opposite page) is the warm-up of every session from now on. Two minutes, clapping, marching. She will need one to ten for the shop next week.
  • If she answers in Greek during the play, do not stop. Repeat her sentence in English as if agreeing, and carry on.

Greek is printed on your sheets only. Her cards and her script are English-only on purpose — the moment a Greek word sits next to an English one, she reads the Greek and the English becomes decoration.

Session 1 · Who Are You?Games
Session 1

Games & the silly versions

Three games

10 min

The Hello Hat

Pass the hat back and forth. Whoever wears it must introduce themselves with a brand-new invented name and age: "My name is Banana. I'm forty-two." Invented names are the trick — it removes the fear of getting her own answer wrong.

10 min

Freeze and Greet

Clap a rhythm and both walk around the room. When you stop clapping, both freeze, turn to each other and do the full greeting. Each round, hold up a different feeling card so the "How are you?" answer changes.

5 min

The Toy Parade

Line up five toys on the sofa. She introduces each one to you: "This is Bunny. She is three." Toys are easier to speak for than herself.

Four silly versions of the same scene

Play these after the straight version has worked twice. The words stay identical; only the world changes. This is where the repetition she needs stops feeling like repetition.

The monster next door

Play B as a monster. Same lines, but "My name is Grrrmp. I'm one hundred years old."

The cat neighbour

Every line starts with "Meow". "Meow — my name is Cleo." She will insist on being the cat.

The robot

Flat voice, one word per second, arms stiff. Great for a child who rushes and swallows words.

The tiny voice / the giant voice

Whole scene whispered, then whole scene shouted. Pure repetition disguised as chaos.

Home mission after this session: Every morning this week, the first words of the day are English. She knocks on your bedroom door: "Good morning! How are you?" You answer, then ask her back.

Session 2 · The Little ShopGames
Session 2

Games & the silly versions

Three games

10 min

Price Hunt

Spread the price tags on the items. You call out "Four francs!" and she must run and grab the right item and shout its name in English. Then swap — she calls, you run. Her calling out is the half that teaches her to produce numbers, not just recognise them.

10 min

The Shopping List

Hand her one of the printed picture lists. She must buy all three items from your shop, in English, and count the money. Four lists are provided so it can run four rounds.

5 min

Cash Register Race

You shout a price, she has three seconds to lay out the exact notes. "Seven francs!" — five plus two. Counting money out loud is the fastest way to fix numbers one to ten.

Four silly versions of the same scene

Play these after the straight version has worked twice. The words stay identical; only the world changes. This is where the repetition she needs stops feeling like repetition.

The Crazy Shop

The shop only sells impossible things: a cloud, a dragon, Monday. Prices are absurd: "One dragon? One hundred francs!"

Sorry, no bananas!

The shopkeeper is out of everything. Whatever the customer asks: "Sorry! No bananas!" The customer must keep asking for something new — that is ten repetitions of the question.

The customer with no money

She tries to pay with a sock, a shoe, a hug. "Can I pay with a sock?" "No! Five francs!"

The shop for giants

Everything is enormous and everything costs one franc. Say every price in a booming voice.

Home mission after this session: She is the cashier at dinner this week. Nobody gets bread until they ask her in English and pay with a play note. She gives the change and says the price.

Session 3 · The Little CaféGames
Session 3

Games & the silly versions

Three games

10 min

Waiter's Memory

You order three things with the pad taken away. She has to repeat the order back correctly before she is allowed to "bring" it. Start with two things, go to four when she is winning.

5 min

Hot or Cold

Hold up a food card. She shouts "The soup is hot!" and blows on her hands, or "The ice cream is cold!" and shivers. The mime is what fixes the adjective.

10 min

Menu Bingo

Both take a bingo card. Take turns being the waiter calling out dishes. First to cross a full row shouts "Bingo! Enjoy your meal!"

Four silly versions of the same scene

Play these after the straight version has worked twice. The words stay identical; only the world changes. This is where the repetition she needs stops feeling like repetition.

The Yuck Café

Use the second menu: soup with a shoe, spaghetti with socks, cold tea with sand. The guest tastes it and says "It is terrible!" This teaches the opposite of delicious.

The waiter who drops everything

Every dish falls. "Oh no! Sorry! One moment, please!" and the order starts again. Free repetition, and she will cry with laughter.

The very hungry guest

She orders ten things in a row. The waiter must repeat the whole list back. Brutal memory work that does not feel like work.

The café for animals

Guest is a dog and can only order things a dog would want. The waiter must stay polite.

Home mission after this session: Saturday breakfast, she is the waiter. She takes the whole family's order on the printed pad, in English, and brings the bill at the end. Pay her in play francs.

Three things worth knowing before you start

  • Reserve the last 25 minutes for the Grand Show — do not let the doctor scenes eat it.
  • Show running order: the doorbell scene, the shop, the café, the clinic. Two minutes each. She plays her favourite role in each, you take the other.
  • Hand her the diploma at the end with a straight face and a handshake. Ceremony is what makes a seven-year-old want session five.

Greek is printed on your sheets only. Her cards and her script are English-only on purpose — the moment a Greek word sits next to an English one, she reads the Greek and the English becomes decoration.

If she freezes

Say the first two words of her line and stop. Nine times out of ten she finishes it. If she still cannot, say the whole line cheerfully as your own and move on — never leave her standing in silence, and never let the scene stop because of a missing word.

The set

A towel on the table is the examination bed. The patient is always a stuffed animal — she speaks for it, which is far easier than speaking for herself. A scarf is the doctor's coat.

Props

Stuffed animals · a towel · prescription pad · appointment cards · waiting numbers

Run it three times. You alone with a toy · she takes the first role · swap. Never fewer than three.

Session 4 · The Animal DoctorGames
Session 4

Games & the silly versions

Three games

10 min

Simon Says

"Simon says touch your nose!" — she does it. "Touch your ear!" without Simon says — she must not move. Fast, physical, and it teaches listening rather than speaking, which is the half that gets neglected.

10 min

The Toy Hospital

Four stuffed animals, each with a printed illness card face down. She draws a card, reads what hurts, examines the patient and fills in a prescription slip.

5 min

Number Three, Please!

Give the toys the printed waiting-room numbers. The doctor calls "Number three, please!" and only that patient may come. Sneaks the numbers back in one last time.

Four silly versions of the same scene

Play these after the straight version has worked twice. The words stay identical; only the world changes. This is where the repetition she needs stops feeling like repetition.

The dragon patient

His nose is too hot. The cure is ten ice creams. "Take ten ice creams, two times a day!"

The doctor who prescribes chocolate

Whatever is wrong, the medicine is chocolate. She will try to make this the real rule of the house.

The patient with one hundred problems

"My head hurts. And my nose hurts. And my foot hurts..." Run through every body word.

The animal that answers back

The stuffed animal speaks for itself and refuses to open its mouth. The doctor must beg politely.

Home mission after this session: Every evening this week, one toy gets sick and she is the doctor. Two minutes, in English, before the bedtime story. Keep the prescription pad by her bed.

Running order

Two minutes per scene, no more. She picks her favourite role in each one and you take the other. Keep the scripts within reach but out of sight — she will want them and will mostly not need them.

The audience's job

Mum claps loudly after every scene and asks one question in English at the end: "What is your name?" Being asked by somebody other than you is the real test of the four sessions.

The home missions

Two minutes a day, every day, with a prop in her hand. This page matters more than any single session — a language grows on daily contact, not on long Sundays. Tick a box each day.

AfterWhat she doesMon–Sun
Session 1Every morning this week, the first words of the day are English. She knocks on your bedroom door: "Good morning! How are you?" You answer, then ask her back.□□□□□□□
Session 2She is the cashier at dinner this week. Nobody gets bread until they ask her in English and pay with a play note. She gives the change and says the price.□□□□□□□
Session 3Saturday breakfast, she is the waiter. She takes the whole family's order on the printed pad, in English, and brings the bill at the end. Pay her in play francs.□□□□□□□
Session 4Every evening this week, one toy gets sick and she is the doctor. Two minutes, in English, before the bedtime story. Keep the prescription pad by her bed.□□□□□□□

The rule that makes missions work

The mission is a job with real consequences inside the family, not homework. Nobody gets bread at dinner until they have asked the cashier in English. If the family plays along, she practises without being asked; if the family forgets, the mission dies in three days.

Keep the box

Everything cut out of this pack — cards, money, menu, prescriptions, the hat — lives in one box that only comes out for English. Never leave the props lying about. Something that appears only for the game keeps the value of the game.

After the four sessionsWhat next

What comes
after session four

Four sessions gets a beginner to about sixty words and eight sentence patterns she can actually use. That is a real result, and it is also fragile. The engine of this pack is not the vocabulary, it is the format — so keep the format and change the world.

Four more worlds, same ninety minutes

5 · The train station

Tickets, "Where are you going?", "To Geneva, please", platform numbers. Numbers get a second life and you can rehearse a real journey.

6 · The birthday party

"Happy birthday!", presents, "Can I have a piece of cake?", "How old are you?" Recycles almost everything from sessions one to three.

7 · The lost dog

Descriptions: big, small, black, white, "Have you seen my dog?" This is where adjectives and colours belong.

8 · The hotel

"A room for two nights, please", floors, keys, breakfast at eight. Combines the shop, the café and the numbers in one scene.

Three things not to do

  • Do not start writing yet.She is learning to read in Greek and in French at the same time. A third spelling system now is one too many. Everything here is spoken; the words on her cards are pictures with a label, not a spelling lesson.
  • Do not test her.No "what is the English for...?" out of the blue. Ask inside a scene or not at all — a question with the hat on is a game, the same question without it is an exam.
  • Do not compete with school French.If she mixes French into English, let it pass. Mixing is a normal stage for a trilingual child, and correcting it is how you teach her to stop volunteering to speak.
